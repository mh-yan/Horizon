## [Lean formalization progress map for disputed Masullo proof sparks debate](https://claude.ai/artifact/CtQyX8qfYAF822bgHJHHE3) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A Lean formalization progress map has been published for Alessandro Masullo's disputed manuscript claiming a proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, tracking how much of the argument has been verified in the Lean proof assistant. The artifact appeared alongside Hacker News commentary questioning the proof's validity and the growing role of agentic AI in mathematics. The item highlights how formal verification tools like Lean are increasingly used to publicly track progress on high-stakes mathematical claims, even disputed ones, and it fuels a broader debate about whether agentic AI can or should play a role in certifying mathematical proofs. It also raises credibility questions about the underlying manuscript, which has been criticized by prominent mathematicians. The progress map is only a tracker, not a verification of correctness; as commenters note, a single unproven gap can invalidate the entire formalization. The manuscript itself is a 940-page draft, and mathematicians including Brian Conrad have said it needs "major revision," while Ravi Vakil has reportedly distanced himself from having reviewed it.

hackernews · Qision · Nov 22, 23:12 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50605045)

**Background**: Lean is an open-source proof assistant and programming language used to formally verify mathematical proofs; it gained attention in 2021 when researchers used it to verify a proof by Peter Scholze in condensed mathematics. The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis is one of the most famous unsolved problems in number theory, and a claimed proof of it would be a major result. Agentic AI refers to AI systems that can autonomously plan and execute multi-step tasks, and there is growing interest in using such systems to assist with mathematical proof formalization.

## [OpenAI Proposes Perplexity as Shared Metric for AI Intelligence](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published an article titled 'Perplexity and Intelligence' on November 22, 2026, presenting OpenAI's internal log-perplexity curve and calling for all frontier labs to adopt a common perplexity metric for measuring AI progress and pacing safety. The article argues that pretraining compression, measured as cross-entropy loss on a fixed held-out set, serves as the 'intelligence substrate' behind model capabilities. If adopted, a shared perplexity metric could enable cross-lab comparisons of model progress and inform industry-wide safety pacing, potentially influencing how AI development is regulated and evaluated. This proposal comes amid growing scrutiny of AI capabilities and could shape standards for transparency and collaboration among frontier labs. The chart uses log perplexity measured against code from internal repositories, which critics argue can be artificially shaped by varying the amount of code in the pretraining corpus. The article does not formally define perplexity, and the metric's relationship to downstream task performance remains debated, especially with the rise of reinforcement learning.

hackernews · acossta · Nov 22, 17:06 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50601525)

**Background**: Perplexity is a metric that quantifies how surprised a language model is when predicting the next token in a sequence; lower perplexity indicates better prediction. It is mathematically related to cross-entropy loss and has long been used to evaluate language models, but its connection to broader intelligence or reasoning ability is not straightforward. OpenAI's proposal seeks to establish a common benchmark for measuring AI progress across the industry.

## [O'Reilly article asks what happened to Silicon Valley, sparking debate](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/what-happened-to-silicon-valley/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Tim O'Reilly published a lecture/article titled 'What Happened to Silicon Valley?' tracing the region's shift from customer-centered innovation to extraction driven by shareholder capitalism, monopoly ambitions, and hype, and linking it to the current AI boom. The piece argues for using mechanism design—reverse game theory—to prioritize human flourishing over corporate profit. The article provides a high-level critique of Silicon Valley's evolution and proposes a framework for realigning incentives, which resonates with growing concerns about capitalism, ZIRP-era excess, and AI-driven incentives. Its discussion of mechanism design offers a constructive lens for policymakers, technologists, and investors to rethink the rules governing innovation. The article suggests that technology's value depends on the rules, incentives, and interfaces around it, and proposes a direction favoring openness. It also connects the shift to the AI boom and its economic stakes, noting that shareholder value has become the dominant optimization target.

hackernews · greenyouse · Nov 22, 19:20 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602947)

**Background**: Mechanism design is a branch of economics and game theory that studies how to construct rules (mechanisms) that produce good outcomes according to a predefined metric, even when the designer lacks full information about players' preferences; it is sometimes called reverse game theory because it starts from a desired outcome and works backward to find a game that implements it. Zero interest-rate policy (ZIRP) refers to periods of very low nominal interest rates, such as in the U.S. from 2008 to 2015 and again from 2020 to 2022, which made it easy for tech companies to grow with cheap capital. The article ties these concepts together to explain Silicon Valley's shift from innovation to optimization for shareholder value.

## [Toyota, Nissan, Honda to Standardize Parts to Cut Costs](https://www.caranddriver.com/news/a74490092/toyota-nissan-honda-standardize-parts-lower-costs/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Toyota, Nissan, and Honda will begin standardizing some vehicle parts in an effort to lower production costs, according to a report by Automotive News. The push extends to Japan's seven automakers, including Mazda, Subaru, Mitsubishi, and Suzuki, as they seek to fend off increasingly competitive Chinese automakers. This strategic shift could help Japanese automakers reduce costs and compete more effectively against Chinese manufacturers like BYD, which benefit from high vertical integration. It signals a broader industry trend toward collaboration and standardization to achieve economies of scale. The standardization effort involves multiple Japanese automakers and aims to simplify parts across brands, but specific parts or timelines have not been disclosed. Historically, automakers like GM and Ford have collaborated on components such as transmissions, though with some differences in implementation.

**Background**: Japanese automakers are facing intense competition from Chinese manufacturers like BYD, which produce a high percentage of components in-house, reducing costs. Standardizing parts across brands can help achieve economies of scale and lower production expenses. This move mirrors past collaborations, such as the GM-Ford 6-speed automatic transmission developed jointly in the 2000s.
