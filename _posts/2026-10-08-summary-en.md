## [Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2026 Awarded to Kagan and Soai for Chirality Work](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2026/press-release/) ⭐️ 9.0/10

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences has awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Henri B. Kagan of Université Paris-Sud, France, and Kenso Soai for their pioneering contributions to chirality and asymmetric autocatalysis. Their work unraveled the mystery of mirror-image molecules and how a chiral product can catalyze its own formation. This recognition highlights foundational chemistry that explains how life's molecules came to exist in only one mirror-image form, a phenomenon known as homochirality. It has broad implications for drug design, materials science, and our understanding of the origin of life. Asymmetric autocatalysis is a reaction in which a chiral product acts as an asymmetric catalyst to produce more of itself in the same absolute configuration, enabling amplification of tiny initial imbalances. This process is key to understanding how a small excess of one enantiomer could lead to the homochirality observed in biological molecules.

hackernews · sasvari · Oct 7, 09:51 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49990470)

**Background**: Chirality is the geometric property of a molecule that cannot be superimposed on its mirror image, like left and right hands. Such mirror-image molecules, called enantiomers, have identical physical properties except for their interaction with other chiral compounds and optical activity. In living organisms, only one enantiomer of chiral biomolecules is typically found, a phenomenon called homochirality. Asymmetric autocatalysis provides a plausible mechanism for the origin of this preference.

## [AI-Assisted Formal Proof Settles Optimal Packing of 11 Squares](https://github.com/Queuingtheorydotcom/11SquaresFormalized) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A new GitHub repository, 11SquaresFormalized, presents an AI-assisted formal proof that establishes the optimal packing of 11 unit squares inside the smallest possible enclosing square. The work formalizes a result that had long been known numerically but lacked a complete, machine-checkable proof. This matters because it demonstrates how AI-assisted formal verification can close long-standing gaps in computational geometry, turning computer-assisted numerical results into rigorous, machine-checked proofs. It signals a broader trend where proof assistants and AI tools are increasingly used to certify mathematical results that were previously only empirically established. The proof follows the standard computer-assisted 'unavoidable set' approach: the enclosing region is divided into 16 small regions such that no two square centers can fit in the same region, yielding about 2000 cases (16 choose 11) that must each be ruled out. The repository is a formalization effort, and community members noted that the README currently lacks figures illustrating the packing.

**Background**: The problem of packing n unit squares into the smallest possible enclosing square is a classic problem in computational geometry. For small n, optimal packings are known, but proving optimality rigorously is difficult because it requires exhaustive case analysis. Formal verification uses proof assistants such as Lean or Coq to mechanically check every logical step, and AI tools can help propose proof tactics while the formal system rejects invalid steps.

Because 64-bit pointers take twice the cache space of 32-bit pointers, reducing pointer size can meaningfully improve memory and cache behavior.
